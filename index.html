The same question, two waysA council says its budget went up 4%, but spending per resident fell. Can both be true?

Answers straight away

No. If the budget went up, the money spent on each resident went up too.

Wrong

Thinks first

  1. Per resident means dividing by the population.
  2. What if the population grew faster than 4%?
  3. 4% more money, 6% more people: 1.04 ÷ 1.06 = 0.98.

Yes, if the population grew faster. With 6% more residents, each gets about 2% less.

Right

The catch

What can go wrong.

When a chatbot gets it wrong, you get a bad answer.

When an agent gets it wrong, it does a bad thing.

  1. 01

    It can be tricked

    Words hidden in a web page, email or document can give it orders. Nobody has solved this.

    2025Researchers showed one crafted email could make Microsoft 365 Copilot leak company data, with no click needed.

  2. 02

    Mistakes become actions

    A wrong answer you can ignore. A wrong email sent, or a wrong purchase, has already happened.

    2025Asked to find cheap eggs, OpenAI's Operator agent bought $31 worth, with priority delivery, without checking first.

  3. 03

    It sees what you let it see

    To act for you it needs your inbox, files and logins. Whatever it can read, it can leak.

    NamedPrivate data, content from strangers and a way to send things out: the “lethal trifecta”. Meta cites it for Muse.

  4. 04

    It chases the goal, not what you meant

    It does whatever gets rewarded, by whatever route works.

    2016A game-playing AI rewarded for points circled one lagoon for ever instead of finishing the race.

The thesis

Why most prompt advice misses

How to think about LLMs.

Prompt engineering has become complex and opaque. But the whole value of LLMs is that you can talk to them. Our training focuses less on tips and more on mental models that are better guides to good results.

Advanced AI training 07 / NN

Before we begin · Non-negotiables

Four key rules.

  • Rule 01

    Check everything

    You are responsible for anything you produce, either for internal or external use. Any input or sources used to create text or multimedia content, whether generated by AI or taken from other sources, must be checked. This is enshrined in our editorial code but applies to all our staff.

  • Rule 02

    Use GenAI within your area of expertise

    Generative AI is particularly risky in situations where it is used outside your field of expertise. It is tempting to use it as a shortcut to avoid resource constraints in other departments or functions. But a lack of expertise means a lack of ability to accurately judge potentially unreliable output. If you do use the technology in this way you must check your work with someone qualified to evaluate it.

  • Rule 03

    Use GenAI as a co-pilot

    GenAI can be used to completely automate a piece of work. But this approach gives too much responsibility to an unreliable machine and makes it tricky to check the output accurately. Instead, work with these tools to help you complete tasks step-by-step. You should still use your own creativity, thought and judgment. Outputs from LLMs may be owned by the LLM provider or not protected by copyright if they are not the product of human effort.

  • Rule 04

    Don't ask GenAI to make decisions or form opinions about people

    GenAI is not a thinking tool and it does not replace your role as an accountable decision maker. Do not use it to make decisions about individuals. It can be used to review and summarise content but only you can make decisions about another person.

Advanced AI training 03 / NN

Getting started · No shortcuts

First, you have to use it.

You can't get your head around what LLMs can do - or grasp their potential at scale - without committing real time to using them. It's non-negotiable. Skip it, and you're the person who tried AI Overviews once, decided GenAI was broken, and walked away.

  • Put time aside, and protect it. Be disciplined about actually turning up.
  • Expect not to finish. The project may never land - but the hours still teach you how they do and don't work.

Don't know where to begin? You have a magic genie in front of you. Ask it. Here's how I started:

My very first prompt

Can you build me a simple app on my machine? I can't code. I will need you to explain everything to me in simple language. Don't assume knowledge on my part. Redirect me if I ask for something stupid or there's a better way.

→Experiment with Canvas in Gemini, and move up to something like Claude Code in their desktop app.

Advanced AI training 03 / NN

How to get started

You have a genie. Ask it for help.

Lots of people struggle to even get started with using LLMs. It might be a fear of lack of technical expertise or, even more commonly, just having no idea about what these machines are capable of.

Before you even get to the building stage, your first conversation with the LLM is probably best spent asking for its help. The conversation you have will likely provide very useful context for you and also the machine when it builds something.

1

Explore

"I'm thinking of building a prompt that can turn Guardian articles into something that's easier to understand for people with reading difficulties. Are there any accepted frames, research or approaches for this in the real world? Can you give me an overview?"

2

Focus

"Which of the frames might be best for my purposes? Ask me questions if you need more context from me."

3

Test

"How can I easily test the prompt we've created? Are there scoring systems for readability that we could use? How much do I need to test to feel like it's working?"

4

Build

"If I like the prompt, is it possible to build something that's more like an app or tool I can share? I don't know anything about coding so you need to be really clear and explain everything."

The magic of LLMs is their ability to infer intent from language. This is also the biggest cause of errors. Don't expect a prompt to work in one shot. Sculpt your way to success through conversation.

"I like the slide showing the graph of adoption but to make it land it needs to animate the lines from left to right."

How to instruct

The mentor & the manager.

Prompt level

Think like a teacher

Teaching is a skillset, and it's exactly what the machine needs. Like a child, it's capable but forgetful - easily distracted, easily led astray. Clear, patient instruction and a steady process matter far more than clever syntax.

Project level

Manage the team

Lead a team on individual tasks that add up to a whole. Break big projects into small, achievable, and verifiable chunks.

Where this leaves us · The competition

Twenty-three minutes.

This morning I asked Fable 5.1 for a scrolling interactive on Gloria Steinem's life: beautiful, in her own words, copyright-free imagery, rigorously sourced, and finished inside two hours. It came back in 23 minutes.

A life, in her own words Open it →
  • Most of it was checking. The bulk of those minutes went on agents researching, double-sourcing and verifying. Every quotation and date carries a numbered citation you can open.
  • Nobody involved was an expert. Not on Steinem, not on the design, not on the code.
  • Look past the finish. The fluency is startling, but it is the judgement that should hold you: what to include, how to structure it, how to write it.
This isn't how we're going to make journalism. But it is what any individual can now make, with no expertise, on a subject they simply find interesting. That is our competition - this year, and far more so next.

Advanced AI training 06 / NN

The state of AI · agency

AI is not just something that happens to us.

We are professionals with clear codes and practices that govern behaviour. And we can make judicious decisions about how we use AI in that frame.

01

Should I use AI?

This is entirely in your hands. But too often it's presented as the only element of control, and one that should be imposed on everyone.

02

Where should I use AI?

Your job is very likely an amalgam of many jobs. Some of those are appropriate for AI and some absolutely depend on your personal control and expertise.

03

How should I use AI?

AI shouldn't - and doesn't have to - be used to execute a whole task. Instead of asking it to create something you can use it to give feedback and suggestions.

In practice

Every slide in this presentation was generated with AI, using Gemini to realise simple formats that worked, ChatGPT to create consistent images and Claude to build the design. All of that integration made the process quicker and the output better. None of it removed or obscured the human expertise behind it.

Why deploying large-scale projects is hard.

  • Identifying use cases What's flexible for one person becomes hard to define for a thousand. Personal use bends to the user; organisational use has to nail the use case first.
  • Reproducibility and audit trail A sole trader can let AI draft an invoice. A 1,000-person firm needs an output that survives an audit.
  • Error consequences at scale A hallucinated paragraph is a shrug in a personal email; a correction in a 200,000-circulation paper.
  • Workforce concerns about displacement Real, live conversations inside most large organisations - including ours.
  • Negative customer reaction AI-produced news is seen as less trustworthy than human-produced news, even with human oversight.

  • The young run ahead. 64% of 16-24s use generative AI, against a small fraction of the over-65s - a 53.6-point age gap, the widest of any divide.
  • Barely a gender gap. Men and women sit just 4.2 points apart. Age is the story; gender isn't.
  • Then it becomes a habit. In the US, close to a third now use AI several times a day - up from a fifth two years ago.
  • Work pulls it up. 41% of employed adults use it against 12.5% of the retired - which is why this all-adult third sits below the workers' four-in-ten-plus.

02 · How they feel

70% of Gen Z find AI useful day to day
  • Indispensable for study. Around 9 in 10 students now use it for coursework, and about half say it has improved their studies.
  • But wary of where it leads. Even the heaviest users are uneasy - roughly half of under-30s expect AI to do society more harm than good.
  • The old aren't hostile - just unsure. Over-65s use it least, and are likeliest to say they don't yet know what to make of it.
